Since the Trinity test in July 1945, eight countries have set off {{n|int}} nuclear test explosions. I put every one of them on a map: where it was, who fired it, how big it was, and whose land or sea it was on. Most were small and underground. But most of the energy came from a few dozen enormous bombs, nearly all of them on remote islands.

Nuclear tests are strangely easy to forget. For most people they live in a handful of black and white films: a cloud rising over an atoll, a flash in the desert. But they were not rare events. In the busiest year there was one every two days.

In 2000 two Swedish researchers, Nils-Olov Bergkvist and Ragnhild Ferm, compiled a list of every known nuclear explosion from 1945 to 1998 for the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute: {{n_sipri|int}} of them, each with a date, a place, a method and an estimate of its size. I added North Korea's six tests since 2006, set aside the two bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, which were not tests, and mapped the rest.

The race to 1962

Testing built up through the 1950s as the Soviet Union and then Britain joined the United States. In 1958 there were {{n_1958|int}} tests. Then the three paused, and for almost three years only France tested. When the Soviet Union broke the pause in September 1961, both sides raced: in {{peak_year|raw}} there were {{peak_n|int}} tests, {{peak_by_country.US|int}} American and {{peak_by_country.USSR|int}} Soviet. The tests of 1961 and 1962 alone released {{yield_61_62_pct|pc}} of all the explosive energy of every nuclear test ever made.

Into the ground

In August 1963 the United States, the Soviet Union and Britain signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty, banning tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water. It did not stop testing. It buried it. Before the treaty {{pre_atm_pct|pc}} of tests were in the open air or underwater; after it {{post_ug_pct|pc}} were underground, and the pace hardly slowed, at about {{avg_1963_92|int}} tests a year until 1992. France and China, which did not sign, kept testing in the atmosphere until 1974 and 1980.

How big

Add up the yields and the {{n|int}} tests released about {{yield_total_mid|mt0}} megatons, the equivalent of {{yield_total_mid|mt0}} million tonnes of TNT (somewhere between {{yield_total_lo|mt0}} and {{yield_total_hi|mt0}}, because many yields are only estimates). But that total is lopsided. The typical test was about {{median_kt|int}} kilotons, smaller than the Hiroshima bomb. The {{mt_tests|int}} tests of a megaton or more make up {{mt_tests_yield_pct|pc}} of the total.

The largest of all was the Soviet test known as Tsar Bomba, dropped by parachute over Novaya Zemlya on {{tsar.date|raw}}: about 50 megatons, {{tsar.pct_total|pc}} of the yield of every test put together. The largest American test, Castle Bravo at Bikini Atoll in 1954, came in at 15 megatons, far more than expected. Its fallout drifted onto the inhabited atolls of Rongelap and Rongerik, which had to be evacuated, and onto a Japanese fishing boat whose crew fell ill.

Whose land

Most tests, {{kinds.0.pct|pc}} of them, were on the testing country's own territory, mostly underground in Nevada, Kazakhstan and Xinjiang. But the big atmospheric bombs were mostly set off a long way from the governments that ordered them. Every one of France's {{c.France.n|int}} tests and Britain's {{c.UK.n|int}} was outside the home country: in the Algerian Sahara, where {{algeria.after_indep|int}} took place after Algeria's independence; on the atolls of French Polynesia; on Aboriginal lands at Maralinga and Emu Field in Australia; on islands that are now part of Kiribati; and in Nevada. The United States held {{marshall.n|int}} tests at Bikini and Enewetak in the Marshall Islands, then a United Nations trust territory under its administration, whose people were moved away first. Those two atolls took {{marshall.pct_us_yield|pc}} of all American test yield.

Put another way: {{island_n|int}} tests, {{island_pct|pc}} of the total, were on islands and atolls, from Novaya Zemlya in the Arctic to Moruroa in the South Pacific. They released {{island_yield_pct|pc}} of all the yield. Novaya Zemlya alone took {{nz_yield_pct|pc}}.

After the test ban

The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty opened for signature in September 1996. It has still not entered into force, but the big testers had already stopped: the Soviet Union in 1990, Britain in 1991, the United States in 1992, France and China in 1996. Since then there have been {{post_ctbt.n|int}} tests: two by India and two by Pakistan in May 1998, and six by North Korea between 2006 and 2017, the last estimated at {{nk.big_lo|int}} to {{nk.big_hi|int}} kilotons. As I write, in 2026, it has been {{years_since_last|int}} years since the last one.

A map of tests is not a map of harm. Fallout travelled far beyond the test sites, and the people who lived downwind, on the atolls and around the sites, and the soldiers and workers who were there, carried the cost in ways this data cannot show. But it does show where the bombs went off, and who decided where.

How I did this

The tests from 1945 to 1998 come from the SIPRI list of nuclear explosions by Bergkvist and Ferm (2000), as shared by data is plural and the TidyTuesday project. North Korea's six tests, and names for some of the largest Soviet tests and the Indian and Pakistani tests, come from Wikipedia. I counted tests as the SIPRI list does, with salvoes of several devices as one test, so my total ({{n|int}}) is lower than some other counts. Yields are given as ranges, often only "less than" some figure, so I used the middle of each range and report the full range beside every total. I classed each site by the status of its land at the time. Circles on the map are sized by the cube root of yield, the way blast damage scales.
