Every nuclear test

Since the Trinity test in the New Mexico desert in July 1945, eight countries have set off {{n|int}} nuclear test explosions. I mapped every one of them: where it was, who fired it, how big it was, and whose land or sea it was on.

The SIPRI list of nuclear explosions, compiled by Nils-Olov Bergkvist and Ragnhild Ferm for the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute and the Swedish Defence Research Establishment, records {{n_sipri|int}} explosions from 1945 to 1998 with a date, a position, a method of firing and an estimate of the yield. I added North Korea's six tests from 2006 to 2017. Two of the explosions in the list were not tests: the bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. They are shown on the map for reference, but every number on this page counts tests only.

Most tests were small, and most were underground. A few dozen enormous atmospheric explosions in the 1950s and early 1960s, nearly all of them on remote islands, account for most of the energy ever released. This page tries to show both.

    02 · WhenThe race to 1962

    The first years were slow: {{c.US.n|int}} tests would eventually be American, but there were only a handful before 1951. Then the Soviet Union, from 1949, and Britain, from 1952, joined in, and the numbers climbed. In 1958 there were {{n_1958|int}} tests. Then the United States, the Soviet Union and Britain paused: there were {{n_1959|int}} tests in 1959 and {{n_1960|int}} in 1960, all of them French. When the Soviet Union broke the pause in September 1961 the result was the busiest two years of testing there has ever been. In {{peak_year|raw}} there were {{peak_n|int}} tests, {{peak_by_country.US|int}} American and {{peak_by_country.USSR|int}} Soviet: one every {{peak_days_per_test|f1}} days. {{peak_atm|int}} of them were in the open air.

    The yield tells an even sharper story. The tests of 1961 and 1962 alone released {{yield_61_62_pct|pc}} of all the explosive energy of every test ever made, most of it in a few dozen Soviet hydrogen bombs over Novaya Zemlya.

    03 · Into the groundAfter the 1963 treaty

    On 5 August 1963 the United States, the Soviet Union and Britain signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty, which banned tests in the atmosphere, in outer space and under water. Testing did not stop. It went underground. Before the treaty, {{pre_atm_pct|pc}} of tests were in the atmosphere or underwater; after it, {{post_ug_pct|pc}} were underground. The number of tests hardly fell: about {{avg_1963_92|int}} a year from 1963 to 1992.

    Two countries did not sign. France carried on testing in the atmosphere until {{c.France.last_atm_year|raw}} ({{post_atm_fr|int}} tests after the treaty, all in French Polynesia), and China until {{c.China.last_atm_year|raw}} ({{post_atm_cn|int}}, at Lop Nur). The last atmospheric test anywhere was Chinese, on {{last_atm.date|raw}}.

    04 · How bigMegatons

    Yields are uncertain, and the SIPRI list gives most of them as a range. Taking the middle of each range, the {{n|int}} tests released about {{yield_total_mid|mt0}} megatons, somewhere between {{yield_total_lo|mt0}} and {{yield_total_hi|mt0}}: the equivalent of {{yield_total_mid|mt0}} million tonnes of TNT. The Soviet Union and the United States account for {{us_ussr_yield_pct|pc}} of it. The typical test was much smaller: the median was {{median_kt|int}} kilotons, and {{under_20_pct|pc}} of tests had an upper estimate of 20 kilotons or less. The {{mt_tests|int}} tests of a megaton or more made up {{mt_tests_yield_pct|pc}} of all the yield.

    One place stands out. Novaya Zemlya, the Arctic islands where the Soviet Union fired its biggest bombs, took {{nz_yield_pct|pc}} of all the yield ever tested, in {{site.novaya.n|int}} tests. Nevada had {{site.nevada.n|int}} tests, {{nevada_pct_n|pc}} of the world's total, but most were small and underground.

    05 · The biggestTsar Bomba and Castle Bravo

    The largest explosion ever set off was the Soviet test known as Tsar Bomba, dropped by parachute over Novaya Zemlya on {{tsar.date|raw}}. Its yield was about 50 megatons, {{tsar.x_hiroshima|int}} times the Hiroshima bomb and {{tsar.pct_total|pc}} of the yield of all tests put together. Of the twenty largest tests, {{top20_ussr|int}} were Soviet and {{top20_us|int}} American, and {{top20_1961_62|int}} took place in 1961 or 1962.

    The largest American test was Castle Bravo, at Bikini Atoll on 1 March 1954 (28 February in the SIPRI list, which uses Greenwich time). Its yield, 15 megatons, was far larger than its designers expected. As the Wikipedia list of tests puts it, the unanticipated yield and a change in the weather spread fallout eastward onto the inhabited Rongelap and Rongerik atolls, which were soon evacuated; the crew of a Japanese fishing boat, the Daigo Fukuryū Maru, also fell ill, and one of them died.

    06 · Whose landColonies, trust territories and islands

    Most tests were on the testing country's own national territory: {{kinds.0.pct|pc}} of them, mostly underground in Nevada, Kazakhstan and Xinjiang. But the biggest atmospheric tests were usually set off far from the capitals that ordered them. {{away_n|int}} tests ({{away_pct|pc}}) were in colonies, overseas territories, trust territories or another country's land, and they released {{away_yield_pct|pc}} of all the yield. And {{island_n|int}} tests ({{island_pct|pc}}) were on islands, which took {{island_yield_pct|pc}} of the yield.

    Every one of France's {{c.France.n|int}} tests was outside metropolitan France: {{algeria.n|int}} in the Algerian Sahara, {{algeria.after_indep|int}} of them after Algeria's independence in July 1962, and {{polynesia.n|int}} on the atolls of Moruroa and Fangataufa in French Polynesia. Every one of Britain's {{c.UK.n|int}} was abroad too: {{australia.n|int}} in Australia, {{kiribati.uk|int}} on islands in the central Pacific that are now part of Kiribati, and {{uk_at_nevada|int}} in Nevada. The United States held {{marshall.n|int}} tests at Bikini and Enewetak in the Marshall Islands, then a United Nations trust territory under American administration; together they released {{marshall.pct_us_yield|pc}} of all American test yield. The Soviet Union and China tested only inside their own borders, though Semipalatinsk lay in the Kazakh Soviet republic, now independent Kazakhstan, and Lop Nur in Xinjiang.

    07 · The test banAfter 1996

    The Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty, which bans all nuclear explosions, opened for signature on 24 September 1996. It has not entered into force. The last American test was in {{c.US.last_year|raw}}, the last Soviet one in {{c.USSR.last_year|raw}}, the last British one in {{c.UK.last_year|raw}}; France stopped in January {{c.France.last_year|raw}} and China in July {{c.China.last_year|raw}}. Since the treaty opened there have been {{post_ctbt.n|int}} tests: {{post_ctbt.by.India|int}} by India and {{post_ctbt.by.Pakistan|int}} by Pakistan in May 1998, and {{post_ctbt.by.NKorea|int}} by North Korea between 2006 and 2017. In Wikipedia's words, there were no declared tests between Pakistan's Chagai-II and North Korea's first test, and none outside North Korea since.

    08 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    The tests. The main source is the list of nuclear explosions from 1945 to 1998 compiled by Bergkvist and Ferm (2000) for SIPRI and the Swedish Defence Research Establishment (FOA), as published by data is plural and the R for Data Science TidyTuesday project (2019). It gives each explosion's date, the testing country, a site code, a position, seismic magnitudes, depth or height, a lower and upper yield estimate, the purpose, a name where one is known and the method of firing. I added North Korea's six tests of 2006 to 2017 from Wikipedia's list of North Korean tests, taking the dates, positions and yield ranges from the page text only. Names for some of the largest Soviet and Chinese tests, and for the Indian and Pakistani tests, come from Wikipedia too.

    Counting. This page counts {{n|int}} tests: {{n_sipri_tests|int}} from the SIPRI list (its {{n_sipri|int}} explosions less Hiroshima and Nagasaki) and {{n_nk|int}} North Korean. Other counts differ. Wikipedia's list gives {{wiki_tests|int}}, because it counts some salvo explosions, British safety trials and Soviet tests differently. Five devices fired together count as one test here, as they do in the SIPRI list.

    Yields are estimates, many of them only an upper bound: {{n_range_0|int}} tests are recorded as "0 to" some figure. I use the middle of each range, and give the sum of the lower and upper estimates beside every total. Totals are dominated by a few dozen megaton tests, whose yields are well known, so the main findings do not depend on how the small tests are counted. Atmospheric here means in the air, on the ground, on a tower, barge or ship, on the water surface or in space; underground includes shafts, tunnels and shallow cratering shots; underwater is shown separately. Land status is the status of the place at the time of the test, as described in the sources below.

    What this does not show. A map of tests is not a map of harm. Fallout drifted far from the sites; people living near Semipalatinsk, in the Marshall Islands, in Nevada's downwind communities, around Maralinga and in French Polynesia were exposed, as were many thousands of service personnel. The doses and their effects are studied elsewhere, notably by UNSCEAR, and are not in this data.
